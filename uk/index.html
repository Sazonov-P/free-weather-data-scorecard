Accuracy check · 67 weather stations across the UK · 2017–2024

Which free weather data can you trust in the UK?

We compared four free global data sets of past weather (ERA5, ERA5-Land, ECMWF IFS and NASA POWER) with measurements at Met Office stations in England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. The Met Office counts its weather day from 9:00 to 9:00 UTC (GMT), so we rebuilt each data set's daily values over the same hours. We also checked which farm tasks free data can handle in place of a station of your own.

By Pavel Sazonov · November 2026

The scorecard

ERA5 and ECMWF IFS share the lead on temperature. Rain has no clear winner.

For each station we compared every source with what the Met Office recorded, day by day for temperature and week by week for rain. A source wins a station when its error there is the lowest.

ERA5 and NASA POWER reconstruct past weather with a weather model and observations. ERA5-Land is a finer version of ERA5 over land. ECMWF IFS is an archive of past forecasts by the European weather centre ECMWF.

ERA5 and ECMWF IFS won almost the same number of stations: 49% and 46% for the daily high, 48% and 43% for the daily low. Their typical errors are 0.06 °C apart for the daily high and 0.03 °C for the daily low. For rain, NASA POWER won the most stations, 37%, but ERA5 has the lower typical error: 5.9 mm a week against 6.3 mm.

Station by station

Where each source wins

Each dot is a station, coloured by the source with the lowest error there. In each cell of a 1° × 0.75° grid, about 65 by 80 km, we took the station with the fewest missing days.

The tie on temperature does not make ERA5 and ECMWF IFS interchangeable: at half of the stations their errors differ by more than 0.29 °C for the daily high and 0.21 °C for the daily low. Where the IFS grid point stands more than 100 m above the station, mostly in the Scottish Highlands, ERA5 had the lower error at all 9 stations, by day and by night. At the 16 stations where ERA5's nearest grid point lies in the sea (we took the nearest one on land), IFS had the lower error in the daily high at 12.

Do you need a weather station?

Free data just passes for daily lows, and fails on frost dates and on which days it rained

A weather station of your own costs money to buy and time to keep calibrated. Task by task, we measured how far off free data would be instead, with Met Office stations as the reference. For each variable we took the source that won the most stations: ERA5 for temperature, NASA POWER for rain. ECMWF IFS for temperature, or ERA5 or ECMWF IFS for rain, would get the same verdicts.

The UK figures hide a difference between England and Scotland. ERA5's degree days were 6% off at the median English station and 21% off at the median Scottish one; with ECMWF IFS it was 7% and 19%. In Scotland ERA5's nights run 1.1 °C too warm, against 0.7 °C in England, and it puts the last spring frost 19 days too early, against 11. So in Scotland free data does not replace a station for degree days. For the daily low there, ECMWF IFS is the better choice (0.6 °C too warm against ERA5's 1.1 °C), except where its grid point stands more than 100 m above the place, where ERA5 had the lower error (map above). The four stations in Northern Ireland look like Scotland's (degree days 20% off, last frost 19 days too early) and the six in Wales more like England's (9% and 10 days), but both groups are too small to be sure.

Typical error: the median over stations of the mean absolute error, 2017–2024, at 65 stations for temperature and 67 for rain. Good enough? The limits are the same in every edition and were set before the UK was measured. Degree days: within 6% of the season total (yes) or 12% (with care). Temperature: within 1.2 °C for a day or 0.6 °C for a month, and less than 0.7 °C too warm or too cool on average (yes), otherwise with care. Last spring frost: within 3 days (yes) or 7 (with care). Monthly rain: within 15% (yes) or 35% (roughly). Rainy days: fewer than 10% of the data's rainy days dry at the station (yes) or 20% (roughly). The daily low passes narrowly: lows run 0.69 °C too warm, just under that limit. With care means usable after removing the steady offset with a local record, which is why temperature is never rated "No".

A small warm offset matters for frost, because a frost is a threshold: ERA5 missed 57% of the April and May frost nights at the median of the 52 stations that have at least one such night a year on average. Frost here is air frost, a low at or below 0 °C at screen height. Last spring frost: the last such night from April to June, at the 62 stations where the station or ERA5 had one; in a year when only one of them had a frost, the other's date counts as 31 March. At the median station it came around 27 April. Degree days: base 10 °C with a 30 °C cap, as for maize; counted from 0 or 5 °C, as for cereals and grass, the season total is larger and the same error a smaller share of it; we did not check those. A day with 1 mm or more counts as rainy. From April to September NASA POWER's monthly totals were off by 25%. Many of these stations feed ERA5 and the ECMWF forecasts, so in a field between stations the error can be larger. We did not check humidity, wind or sunshine, which disease models, spraying and irrigation need.

Error at your field

Nearby stations help estimate rain error, but not temperature error

The errors above are for the typical station. To estimate what a field can expect, we predicted each station's error from the stations around it, leaving that station out. Across all five editions (the UK, the US, Europe, Canada and Australia; one page each), the estimate of weekly rain error came 23% closer to the actual error than the typical value of the station's region. This estimates the size of the error; it does not correct the rain data. For temperature, this method did not improve on the regional value reliably. The form therefore gives the typical temperature error of the nearest station's nation. In England the estimate came 10% closer, in Scotland 48%. In Wales, where we have only six stations, it missed the actual error by more than the typical value for Wales did, so read the map there as a rough guide.

A trap in the timestamps

The Met Office's weather day runs from 9:00 to 9:00 UTC (GMT)

The Met Office ends its weather day at 9:00 UTC, which is 9 a.m. in winter and 10 a.m. on summer clocks. Monday's high and Monday's rain cover the 24 hours from 9:00 UTC on Monday; Monday's low covers the 24 hours to 9:00 UTC on Monday, that is, the night before. We rebuilt every data set's daily values over the same hours.

To check the hours, we moved the end of the day hour by hour and measured how far ERA5's daily rain landed from the gauges each time. The error was smallest with the day ending at 9:00 UTC, as the Met Office documents; that hour came out best at 54 of 67 stations. A plain calendar day, midnight to midnight UTC, made the daily rain error 40% larger and the error of the daily low 34% larger. For the daily low, the Met Office's hours also gave the smallest error. For the daily high we judge the hours by the spread of ERA5's errors (their standard deviation), because its cool days shift the average error; there the Met Office's hours came within 1% of the smallest spread.

Systematic bias

Every source runs cool by day and warm by night

At the typical station, every source underestimates the daily high, by 0.92 to 1.33 °C, and overestimates the daily low, by 0.54 to 0.75 °C. The cool days cost degree days; the warm nights hide frosts. ERA5's highs run 1.4 °C too cool in summer and 0.6 °C too cool in winter.

Part of the daytime gap is height: the typical ERA5 grid point stands 37 m higher than its station, and the air cools with height. Adjusting ERA5 to the station's height with the standard lapse rate, 6.5 °C per km, cut its typical error in the daily high from 1.16 to 0.88 °C; with every source adjusted the same way, ERA5 won 69% of stations. For the daily low it made things worse, 1.02 to 1.24 °C, because the nights already run warm.

Part of the gap comes from how daily values are built: a source gives one value per hour, while a station records the true peak and the true low, which often fall between the hours. At German stations, in our European edition, that accounted for about half of ERA5's bias, in the daily high and in the daily low.

The UK and four other regions

ERA5 and NASA POWER win the most stations in the UK too, but only just

Counted by stations won, ERA5 leads on temperature and NASA POWER on rain in all five editions (the UK, the US, Europe, Canada and Australia). In the UK their lead is the narrowest, except on daily highs, where Australia's race was as close. ERA5's typical error in the daily low is the smallest of the five: 1.0 °C, against 1.5 in the US, 1.2 in Europe, 1.6 in Canada and 1.4 in Australia. Its error in the daily high, 1.2 °C, is mid-range among the five (1.1 to 1.3 °C).

NASA POWER's monthly rain error is the largest of the five in millimetres, 18 mm, and the smallest as a share of the month's total: 22%, against 25% in the US, 24% in Europe, 35% in Canada and 36% in Australia. The UK stations are also the wettest of the five. Degree days above 10 °C go the other way: ERA5's season total is 11% off, against 3% to 5% in the other editions. The UK season adds up to only 765 degree days at the median station, and the days run cool.

How we checked

Method and limits

  • Reference: Met Office stations in MIDAS Open (version 202607, CEDA Archive), 2017–2024: daily max and min temperature and rain. We used only records that the Met Office did not mark as suspect or estimated; a trace of rain counts as zero.
  • Only stations that observe around the clock, which we recognise by hourly rain for the whole period: the Met Office's staffed and automatic stations. Volunteer climate stations were left out: the MIDAS guide says a few of them observe at other hours, and the archive files them all at 9:00 UTC.
  • Temperature: the highest and lowest value of each 12 hours, ending at 9:00 and 21:00 UTC, joined into a day. Rain: the 12-hour totals of the same stations, or 24 hourly totals where one is missing; MIDAS Open holds almost no daily totals for automatic stations. At 10 stations that have them, for April to September 2020, our totals matched the Met Office's own within 0.2 mm on 88% of days at the median station, dry days included; their correlation was 0.994 or higher, and 0.40 at most when shifted by a day. All days run from 9:00 to 9:00 UTC (chart above), and every source's daily values were rebuilt from its hourly values over the same hours, NASA POWER included. The Met Office's readings are taken ten minutes before the hour.
  • All of the UK, islands included; stations above 500 m, on mountains, were left out, and so would be stations that moved more than 2 km or 50 m in height (none did). One station per 1° × 0.75° cell: the one with the fewest missing days, with at least 90% of days in 7 of 8 years for every variable. Wales has six stations and Northern Ireland four, so their figures are rough.
  • Near the coast the nearest grid point over land can be far away. Fair Isle and the Isles of Scilly count for rain only, because ERA5-Land has no land point near them; there the other sources are read at sea points, and so is ERA5 at Lerwick. At the 16 stations where ERA5's nearest grid point lies in the sea we took the nearest one on land; at 8 of them it is 21 to 54 km away.
  • ECMWF IFS here is Open-Meteo's archive of the high-resolution forecast. It starts in 2017, which sets the period.
  • Each source is read at the nearest grid point over land: the raw grid value, without interpolation and without height adjustment. Open-Meteo's own API adjusts temperature for height by default, so downloads from there differ. All sources are compared on the same days.
  • A grid value describes an area 9 to 50 km across, while a station measures one point. Part of every error comes from this mismatch.
  • Many of these stations send their readings to the forecast centres, and ERA5 and the ECMWF forecasts use them. NASA POWER's rain is MERRA-2's, corrected with gauge data, but the correction fades out between 42.5° and 62.5° north, so the UK gets only part of it. In a field between stations, errors may be larger.
  • Rain gauges here tip in steps of 0.2 mm and catch too little in strong wind, snow above all, so part of the rain error, and of the 3% to 16% by which the sources exceed the gauges over the year, comes from the gauges.

Accuracy check · 122 weather stations on Canadian farmland · 2017–2024

Which free weather data can you trust in Canada?

We compared four free global data sets (ERA5, ERA5-Land, ECMWF IFS and NASA POWER) with the daily records of Environment and Climate Change Canada on the farmland of the Prairies, Ontario and Quebec. Canada's weather service closes each day at 6:00 UTC, so we rebuilt each data set's daily values over the same hours. We also checked which farm tasks free data can handle in place of a station of your own.

Do you need a weather station?

Free data is good enough for degree days, not for frost or rain

A weather station of your own costs money to buy and time to keep calibrated. We measured what free data gets wrong in its place, task by task, at the stations of Canada's weather service. For each variable we took the best free global source: ERA5 for temperature, NASA POWER for rain.

Typical error: the median over stations of the mean absolute error, 2017–2024, at 122 stations; for rainy days, the share of days. Last spring frost: the last night at or below 0 °C from April to June; at the median station it came around May 18. Degree days: base 10 °C with a 30 °C cap. A day with 1 mm or more counts as rainy. The rain rows cover the whole year, snow included, and gauges catch less snow in wind; from April to September NASA POWER's monthly totals were off by 32%. Many of these stations feed ERA5 and the ECMWF forecasts, so in a field between stations the error can be larger. In our US test, ERA5 was as accurate on daily highs at farm stations outside the national archive as at airports, and slightly less accurate on daily lows. We did not check humidity, wind or sunshine, which disease models, spraying and irrigation need.

Error at your field

The rain error at your field can be mapped. The temperature error cannot.

The errors above are for the typical station. To see what a particular field can expect, we estimated each station's error from the stations around it, as if it were a new field. For rain this works: the error of weekly totals follows the local climate, larger where it rains more, and the estimate from nearby stations came 23% closer to a station's actual error than the typical value of its province. For temperature it does not: the error depends on the spot itself, a hollow, a slope or a town nearby, so neighbors tell you no more than the typical value of your province.

A trap in the timestamps

Canada's weather day ends at 6:00 UTC

Environment and Climate Change Canada closes the day at 6:00 UTC at stations that observe around the clock. That is midnight in Saskatchewan and Manitoba, 1 a.m. in Ontario and Quebec and 11 p.m. in Alberta, in standard time. We rebuilt every data set's daily values over the same hours. Volunteer stations read their instruments in the morning and the evening, so their days end at other hours; we left them out.

To check the hours, we moved the end of the day hour by hour and measured how far ERA5's daily rain landed from the gauges each time. The error was smallest with the day ending at 6:00 UTC, as the service documents; that hour came out best at 77 of 122 stations. For the daily high and low, the service's hours also gave the smallest spread of errors.

Systematic bias

Days run cool, nights run warm

At the typical station, every source underestimates the daily high, by 0.4 to 0.8 °C, and overestimates the daily low, by 1.1 to 2.2 °C. ERA5-Land is the warmest at night, 2.2 °C too warm. The night-time error matters most for frost: a source that is too warm at dawn misses frosts that the field gets. The same pattern showed up in the US, Germany and France.

Canada, the US, Europe and Australia

The same winners as in the US, Europe and Australia

ERA5 led on temperature and NASA POWER on rain in all four tests. Canadian nights are harder: ERA5's typical error of the daily low is 1.6 °C, against 1.5 in the US and 1.2 in Europe. Rain errors look smaller in millimetres, because the Prairies are dry, but as a share of the month's total they are larger: 35% for NASA POWER, against 25% in the US and 24% in Europe.

How we checked

Method and limits

  • Reference: Environment and Climate Change Canada daily climate observations, 2017–2024: daily max and min temperature and total precipitation. Trace precipitation counts as zero; estimated and missing values were dropped.
  • Only stations that observe around the clock, which we recognize by hourly data for the whole period. Their day runs from 6:00 to 6:00 UTC (chart above), and every source's daily values were rebuilt from its hourly values over the same hours, NASA POWER included.
  • The farm belt: Alberta, Saskatchewan and Manitoba up to 55° north, Ontario and Quebec up to 49° north. This is a simple latitude cut, not a crop map. One station per 1° × 0.75° cell: the most complete one, with at least 90% of days in 7 of 8 years for every variable.
  • ECMWF IFS here is Open-Meteo's archive of the high-resolution forecast. It starts in 2017, which sets the period.
  • Each source is read at the nearest grid point over land, without interpolation, and all sources are compared on the same days.
  • A grid value describes an area 9 to 50 km across, while a station measures one point. Part of every error comes from this mismatch, for every source alike.
  • Many of these stations send their readings to the forecast centers, and ERA5 and the ECMWF forecasts use them. NASA POWER rain is corrected with gauge data. In a field between stations, errors may be larger.
  • Snow is measured as water in the gauge, and gauges catch less snow in wind. Winter precipitation errors partly reflect the gauges, not only the sources.

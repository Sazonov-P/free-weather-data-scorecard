Which free weather data can you trust in Germany and France?

We compared four free global data sets (ERA5, ERA5-Land, ECMWF IFS and NASA POWER) with the daily records of the German and French national weather services. Each service ends its day at its own hours, so we cut every data set into the same windows before comparing.

Station by station

Where each source wins

Each dot is a station, colored by the source with the lowest error there. We took the most complete station in each French département and in each cell of a 1° × 0.75° grid over Germany, which is about the size of a département.

A trap in the timestamps

Rain days run from 6 to 6

Ask DWD or Météo-France for Monday's rain and you get what fell between 6:00 UTC on Monday and 6:00 UTC on Tuesday. Temperature days differ by country. DWD takes Monday's high and low between midnight and midnight UTC. Météo-France takes Monday's high from 6:00 UTC on Monday to 6:00 UTC on Tuesday, and Monday's low from 18:00 UTC on Sunday to 18:00 UTC on Monday. The free data sets come as hourly values in UTC, so we cut each of them into the service's own windows.

Does it matter? We moved the end of the day hour by hour and measured how far ERA5's daily rain landed from the gauges each time. The error was smallest with the day ending at 6:00 UTC, as both services document; that hour came out best at 59 of 80 German and 62 of 94 French stations. A plain calendar day, midnight to midnight in local winter time, made the daily rain error 23% larger in Germany and 22% larger in France. Temperature is less sensitive, because the day's high and low usually fall well inside either window: calendar days added at most 6% to ERA5's error.

Systematic bias

Days run cool, nights run warm

At the typical station every source underestimates the daily high, by 0.9 to 1.1 °C. ERA5, ERA5-Land and the ECMWF forecasts overestimate the daily low, by 0.6 to 0.8 °C. NASA POWER's daily low is right on average, but it scatters the most, so its typical error is still the largest. The same pattern showed up in the US.

Height

In the hills, correct for height

A grid value stands for the average height of its cell. At most stations the difference is small: the median ERA5 grid point sits 17 m above its station. In the mountains it reaches several hundred meters either way, and more than 1,000 m at Luchon, a valley station in the Pyrenees. By day the air cools with height, so ERA5's daily high comes out about 0.7 °C cooler for every 100 m the grid point stands above the station. Height explains 85% of the station-to-station spread in that bias, but not the bias itself: where heights match, ERA5's high still runs 0.7 °C cool. At night cold air pools in valleys, and the link is weaker, 0.3 °C per 100 m.

Adjusting ERA5 to the station's height with the standard lapse rate, 6.5 °C per km, cut its typical error of the daily high from 1.09 to 0.99 °C. For the daily low it made things slightly worse: 1.19 to 1.24 °C.

Europe and the US

The same winners as in the US

Our US test of 172 airport and reference stations picked the same sources: ERA5 for temperature and NASA POWER for rain. There, too, the 9 km grids of ERA5-Land and the ECMWF forecasts did not beat ERA5. ERA5-Land is not fitted to station readings the way ERA5 is, which may be part of the reason. ERA5's lead is narrower in Europe, and its error of the daily low is smaller than in the US.

How we checked

Method and limits

  • Reference: daily station records of the national weather services, 2017–2024. Germany: DWD Climate Data Center, daily max and min temperature and rain. France: Météo-France basic daily climate data for mainland France and Corsica; values flagged as doubtful were dropped.
  • Each service closes its day at its own hours (chart above). Every source was cut into the same windows from its hourly values, NASA POWER included. German stations count only if their metadata show these windows for the whole period.
  • One station per French département and per 1° × 0.75° cell in Germany: the most complete one, with at least 90% of days in 7 of 8 years for every variable. Stations that moved more than 2 km or 50 m in height during the period were left out.
  • Each source is read at the nearest grid point over land, without interpolation, and all sources are compared on the same days. Helgoland has no land point nearby in ERA5-Land, so it counts for rain only.
  • A grid value describes an area 9 to 50 km across, while a station measures one point. Part of every error comes from this mismatch, for every source alike.
  • Many of these stations send their readings to the forecast centers, and ERA5 and the ECMWF forecasts use them. NASA POWER rain is corrected with gauge data. In a field between stations, errors may be larger.
  • Two countries and eight years. Mountains, coasts and southern Europe are covered by few stations.

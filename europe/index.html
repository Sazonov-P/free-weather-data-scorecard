Accuracy check · 261 weather stations in seven countries · 2017–2024

Which free weather data can you trust in Europe?

We compared four free global data sets (ERA5, ERA5-Land, ECMWF IFS and NASA POWER) with the daily records of the national weather services of Germany, France, Austria, Switzerland, Czechia, Denmark and the Netherlands. Most of the services do not count their days from midnight to midnight local time, so we rebuilt each data set's daily values over each service's own hours before comparing. We also checked which farm tasks free data can handle in place of a station of your own.

Do you need a weather station?

Free data is good enough for degree days, not for frost or daily rain

A weather station of your own costs money to buy and time to keep calibrated. We measured what free data gets wrong in its place, task by task, at the stations of the seven weather services. For each variable we took the best free global source: ERA5 for temperature, NASA POWER for rain.

Austria splits in two. At 11 of its 23 stations the ERA5 grid point is within 300 m of the station's height, and ERA5 missed 5% of the season's degree days, as in Germany. The other 12 sit in Alpine valleys, 300 m or more below the grid point: there ERA5 missed 42% of the degree days and put the last spring frost 18 days late. In such valleys free data does not replace a station.

Last spring frost: the last night at or below 0 °C from April to June, at the 255 stations that had one; at the median station it came around April 22. Degree days: base 10 °C with a 30 °C cap. We did not check humidity, wind or sunshine, which disease models, spraying and irrigation need.

Error at your field

Nearby stations help estimate rain error. Temperature needs a local check.

The errors above are for the typical station. To estimate what a field can expect, we predicted each station's error from the stations around it, leaving that station out. Across all four editions, the estimate of weekly rain error came 23% closer to the actual error than the typical value of the station's state, country or province. This estimates the size of the error; it does not correct the rain data. For temperature, this method did not improve on the regional value reliably. The form therefore gives the typical temperature error of the nearest station's region.

A trap in the timestamps

The services end their days at different hours

Ask DWD, Météo-France, GeoSphere Austria or ČHMÚ for Monday's rain and you get what fell between 6:00 UTC on Monday and 6:00 UTC on Tuesday. KNMI and MeteoSwiss count Monday from midnight to midnight UTC, and DMI from midnight to midnight Danish time. For temperature, the hours differ even more. DWD, KNMI and MeteoSwiss take Monday's high and low from midnight to midnight UTC. Météo-France takes Monday's high from 6:00 UTC on Monday to 6:00 UTC on Tuesday, and Monday's low from 18:00 UTC on Sunday to 18:00 UTC on Monday. GeoSphere Austria takes both from 18:00 UTC on Sunday to 18:00 UTC on Monday, ČHMÚ from 20:00 to 20:00 UTC. The free data sets come as hourly values in UTC, so we rebuilt their daily values over the same hours each service uses.

To see how much this matters, we moved the end of the day hour by hour and measured how far ERA5's daily rain landed from the gauges each time. For six of the seven services the error was smallest with the day ending at the hour the service documents. For ČHMÚ it was smallest an hour later, by half a percent; ČHMÚ's own hourly rain adds up to its daily totals over 6:00 to 6:00 UTC, so we kept those hours. A plain calendar day, midnight to midnight in local winter time, made the daily rain error 23% larger in Germany, 22% in France, 18% in Austria and 22% in Czechia. In the Netherlands and Switzerland, whose services end the day at midnight UTC, an hour after local midnight in winter, calendar days added only 1% and 1%. For temperature, calendar days added at most 8% to ERA5's error, because the day's high and low usually fall well inside either window.

Systematic bias

Days run cool, nights run warm

At the typical station, every source underestimates the daily high by 0.9 to 1.1 °C. ERA5, ERA5-Land and the ECMWF forecasts overestimate the daily low by 0.6 to 0.8 °C. NASA POWER's daily low is right on average, but it scatters the most, so its typical error is still the largest. The same pattern showed up in the US. Austria and Switzerland are the exception at night: about half of their stations sit 300 m or more below the grid points, most of them in Alpine valleys, and there every source runs cold by night as well as by day.

Part of the gap comes from how daily values are built. A source gives one value per hour, while the station records the true peak and the true low, which often fall between the hours. At 79 German stations, the highest of the station's own 24 hourly readings was on average 0.43 °C below its daily maximum, and the lowest was 0.35 °C above its daily minimum. That is 55% of ERA5's daytime bias in Germany and 48% of its night-time bias, and it affects every hourly source the same way.

Height

In the hills, correct for height

A grid value stands for the average height of its cell. At most stations the difference is small. The median ERA5 grid point sits 21 m above its station. In the mountains it reaches several hundred meters either way, and more than 1,000 m at Luchon in the Pyrenees, at Innsbruck and Obervellach in Austria and at Aigle, Cevio and Grono in Switzerland, all valley stations. By day the air cools with height, so ERA5's daily high comes out about 0.6 °C cooler for every 100 m the grid point stands above the station. Height explains 89% of the station-to-station spread in that bias. Where the heights match, ERA5's high still runs 0.7 °C cool, 0.43 °C of it from the hourly sampling above. At night cold air pools in valleys, and the link is weaker, at 0.5 °C per 100 m.

Adjusting ERA5 to the station's height with the standard lapse rate, 6.5 °C per km, cut its typical error of the daily high from 1.17 to 1.01 °C. For the daily low the error grew slightly, from 1.24 to 1.30 °C.

Europe and the US

The same winners as in the US

Our US test of 172 airport and reference stations picked the same sources: ERA5 for temperature and NASA POWER for rain. ERA5's lead is narrower in Europe, and Denmark goes its own way: there the ECMWF forecasts won most stations on temperature, and ERA5 won on rain, with NASA POWER far behind. ERA5's error of the daily low is smaller than in the US in every country but Austria and Switzerland, where many stations sit in Alpine valleys far below the grid points; there its error of the daily high is about twice that in Germany.

How we checked

Method and limits

  • Reference: daily station records of the national weather services, 2017–2024. Germany: DWD Climate Data Center, daily max and min temperature and rain. France: Météo-France basic daily climate data for mainland France and Corsica; values flagged as doubtful were dropped. Austria: GeoSphere Austria daily station data; only values the service checked and left unchanged. Switzerland: MeteoSwiss SwissMetNet daily values. Czechia: ČHMÚ daily climate data; only values marked as good. Denmark: DMI climate data; only values the service checked by hand. Netherlands: KNMI daily data.
  • Each service closes its day at its own hours (chart above). Every source's daily values were rebuilt from its hourly values over the same hours, NASA POWER included. German stations count only if their metadata show these hours for the whole period.
  • One station per French département and per 1° × 0.75° cell in the other countries: the most complete one, with at least 90% of days in 7 of 8 years for every variable. Stations that moved more than 2 km or 50 m in height during the period were left out; in Austria a move starts a new station record, so we used single-site records only. Outside Germany and France only stations at or below 1,000 m count, and in the Netherlands none of the stations at sea.
  • ECMWF IFS here is Open-Meteo's archive of the high-resolution forecast. It starts in 2017, which sets the period.
  • Each source is read at the nearest grid point over land, without interpolation, and all sources are compared on the same days. Helgoland and Anholt have no land point nearby in ERA5-Land, so they count for rain only.
  • A grid value describes an area 9 to 50 km across, while a station measures one point. Part of every error comes from this mismatch, for every source alike.
  • Many of these stations send their readings to the forecast centers, and ERA5 and the ECMWF forecasts use them. NASA POWER rain is corrected with gauge data. In a field between stations, errors may be larger.
  • Seven countries and eight years. Mountains, coasts and southern Europe are covered by few stations or none.

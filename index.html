Accuracy check · 374 US weather stations · 2015–2024

Which free weather data can you trust?

We compared four free global data sets (ERA5, ERA5-Land, ECMWF IFS and NASA POWER) with daily observations at US weather stations. Then we tested the leaders against two free station-based grids, NOAA nClimGrid and gridMET, on 100 farm weather stations that NOAA's grid never saw. We also checked which farm tasks free data can handle in place of a station of your own.

Do you need a weather station?

Free data is good enough for degree days, not for daily rain

A weather station of your own costs money to buy and time to keep calibrated. We measured what free data gets wrong in its place, task by task, at 100 farm weather stations that are not in NOAA's station archive (how we tested). For each variable we took the best free global source: ERA5 for temperature, NASA POWER for rain.

Error at your field

The rain error at your field can be mapped. The temperature error cannot.

The errors above are for the typical station. To see what a particular field can expect, we estimated each station's error from the stations around it, as if it were a new field. For rain this works: the error of weekly totals follows the local climate, larger where it rains more, and the estimate from nearby stations came 23% closer to a station's actual error than the typical value of its state. For temperature it does not: the error depends on the spot itself, a hollow, a slope or a town nearby, so neighbors tell you no more than the typical value of your state.

Grid spacing

A finer grid did not mean a smaller error

ERA5-Land and the ECMWF forecast model both run on a 9 km grid, almost three times finer than ERA5. Both missed the daily high by about 0.4 °C more than ERA5 did, including in the hills of eastern Washington and in California's Central Valley.

Systematic bias

Days run cool, nights run warm

At the typical station, every source underestimates the daily high and overestimates the daily low. The night-time error matters most for frost: a source that is 1.1 °C too warm at dawn misses frosts that the field gets. The gap is largest at rural reference stations, which cool faster at night than airports.

A trap in the timestamps

Check when the day ends

Airport and reference stations report each day from midnight to midnight, local standard time. The free grids do not. nClimGrid's "Tuesday" ends around 7 a.m. on Tuesday, because many of its inputs are volunteer observers who read their gauges in the morning. gridMET's documentation says its day ends at midnight Mountain time. In the data it ends five hours later, at 6 a.m. Central time (12:00 UTC). A day ending at that hour gave the closest match to observed rain at {{gm_n_best}} of {{gm_n}} farm stations. Compare a grid with a station on the wrong day and Monday's storm lands on Tuesday.

Independent test, 100 farm stations

In the US, free station-based grids win on rain

The station grids are built from the same kind of stations we used to score the global sources, so they would look good on them. For a fair test we used two farm networks whose stations are not in GHCN-Daily, the NOAA archive that nClimGrid is built from. They are the Iowa State University soil moisture network and the North Dakota Agricultural Weather Network, which also runs stations in Minnesota and Montana. No station we used sits within 1 km of a GHCN-Daily station that measures the same variable.

Temperature is less clear-cut. The answer depends on how you define the daily high. Built from 24 hourly readings, it comes out about 0.6 °C lower than the official daily maximum of the same station, and that favors sources whose daily high is also taken from hourly values, such as ERA5.

Our experiment

Station corrections improve temperature but not rain

We also built a blend. For any point, it removes each source's typical bias using nearby stations and then weights the sources so that their errors partly cancel. We fixed the method on 172 stations and tested it on 102 stations it had never seen. It cut the error of daily highs by 9%, of daily lows by 22% and of frost-day counts by a third. Rain did not improve, because the sources tend to miss the same storms.

How we checked

Method and limits

  • Reference: NOAA GHCN-Daily for airport (ASOS) and USCRN stations, 2015–2024, quality-controlled; days are midnight to midnight, local standard time. Hourly sources were aggregated to the same day.
  • Each source is read at the nearest grid point over land, without interpolation. All sources are compared on the same days. ECMWF IFS is an archive of high-resolution forecasts that starts in 2017, so the ranking covers 2017–2024.
  • A grid value describes an area 4 to 50 km across, while a station measures one point. Part of every error comes from this mismatch, for every source alike.
  • ERA5 assimilates surface temperature observations and NASA POWER rain is corrected with gauge data, which gives both a head start in the data-rich US. Where stations are sparse, the ranking may change.
  • Ten states only: IA, IL, NE, MN, KS, ND, eastern WA, California's Central Valley, TX and GA. Mountains and coasts are under-represented.
  • The farm-station test ran over April to October 2017–2024. Hourly readings narrow the daily range, so temperature results are shown both ways.

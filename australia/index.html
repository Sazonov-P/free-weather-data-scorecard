Accuracy check · 252 weather stations across Australia · 2017–2024

Which free weather data can you trust in Australia?

We compared four free global data sets (ERA5, ERA5-Land, ECMWF IFS and NASA POWER) with the daily records of Bureau of Meteorology stations across Australia. The Bureau closes each day at 9 a.m. local time, so we rebuilt each data set's daily values over the same hours, station by station. We also checked which farm tasks free data can handle in place of a station of your own.

Do you need a weather station?

Free data is good enough for degree days, not for frost or rain

A weather station of your own costs money to buy and time to keep calibrated. We measured what free data gets wrong in its place, task by task, at the Bureau's stations. For each variable we took the best free global source: ERA5 for temperature, NASA POWER for rain. Seasons are southern: the growing season here runs from October to March.

Typical error: the median over stations of the mean absolute error, 2017–2024, at 251 stations; for rainy days, the share of days. Last spring frost: the last night at or below 0 °C from October to December, at the 47 stations that had one; at the median of them it came around October 21. Earlier frosts, in August and September, are outside this window. Degree days: base 10 °C with a 30 °C cap, summed over October to March. A day with 1 mm or more counts as rainy. Many of these stations feed ERA5 and the ECMWF forecasts, so in a field between stations the error can be larger. We did not check humidity, wind or sunshine, which disease models, spraying and irrigation need.

Error at your field

The rain error at your field can be mapped. The temperature error cannot.

The errors above are for the typical station. To see what a particular field can expect, we estimated each station's error from the stations around it, as if it were a new field. For rain this works: the error of weekly totals follows the local climate, larger where it rains more, and the estimate from nearby stations came 22% closer to a station's actual error than the typical value of its state. For temperature it does not: the error depends on the spot itself, a hollow, a slope or a town nearby, so neighbors tell you no more than the typical value of your state.

A trap in the timestamps

Australia's weather day ends at 9 a.m. local time

The Bureau of Meteorology reads its rain gauges and thermometers at 9 a.m. local clock time, daylight saving time included. Monday's rain and Monday's low cover the 24 hours to 9 a.m. on Monday; the high read at that time is booked to the day before, so Monday's high covers the 24 hours from 9 a.m. on Monday. Australia has three time zones, one of them half an hour off the hour, and four states and the Australian Capital Territory move their clocks in summer, so we rebuilt every data set's daily values over each station's own hours, day by day.

To check the hours, we moved the end of the day hour by hour and measured how far ERA5's daily rain landed from the gauges each time. The error was smallest with the day ending at 9 a.m. local time, as the Bureau documents; that hour came out best at 106 of 252 stations, more than any other. A plain calendar day, midnight to midnight, made the daily rain error 58% larger. For the daily high and low, the Bureau's hours also gave the smallest spread of errors.

Systematic bias

Nights run warm. Days run cool, except in NASA POWER

At the typical station, ERA5, ERA5-Land and the ECMWF forecasts underestimate the daily high by 1.1 to 1.4 °C, while NASA POWER's daily high is right on average, 0.1 °C off. Every source overestimates the daily low, by 0.9 to 1.6 °C. The night-time error matters most for frost: a source that is too warm at dawn misses frosts that the field gets. In Europe NASA POWER was the other way round: nights right on average, days too cool.

Australia, the US, Europe and Canada

The same leaders as in the US, Europe and Canada

ERA5 led on temperature and NASA POWER on rain in all four tests. Australia's daily highs are the hardest: ERA5's typical error is 1.3 °C, against 1.1 in the US, 1.2 in Europe and 1.1 in Canada. Its error of the daily low, 1.4 °C, is smaller than in the US (1.5) and Canada (1.6) and larger than in Europe (1.2). As a share of the month's total, the rain error is as large as in Canada: 36% for NASA POWER, against 25% in the US, 23% in Europe and 35% in Canada.

How we checked

Method and limits

  • Reference: daily records of Bureau of Meteorology stations from SILO's patched point datasets, 2017–2024: daily max and min temperature and rain. We kept only the values SILO marks as the station's own observation and dropped everything SILO fills in from nearby stations or by interpolation.
  • Each station's day runs from 9 a.m. to 9 a.m. local clock time (chart above), daylight saving time included, and every source's daily values were rebuilt from its hourly values over the same hours, NASA POWER included. Where local time is off the full hour, in South Australia, the Northern Territory, Broken Hill and Eucla, hourly data cannot split the hour, so those days start up to 45 minutes off.
  • All states and the Northern Territory, mainland and Tasmania. One station per 1° × 0.75° cell: the most complete one, with at least 90% of days in 7 of 8 years for every variable.
  • Seasons are southern: the growing season runs from October to March, and frost windows are those of the northern editions moved by six months.
  • ECMWF IFS here is Open-Meteo's archive of the high-resolution forecast. It starts in 2017, which sets the period.
  • Each source is read at the nearest grid point over land, without interpolation, and all sources are compared on the same days.
  • A grid value describes an area 9 to 50 km across, while a station measures one point. Part of every error comes from this mismatch, for every source alike.
  • Many of these stations send their readings to the forecast centers, and ERA5 and the ECMWF forecasts use them. NASA POWER rain is corrected with gauge data. In a field between stations, errors may be larger.
